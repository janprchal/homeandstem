underground in cool soil before the ground freezes, so anything planted now has time to

## What to Look for When Buying Fall Bulbs

### Bulb size and firmness

Bigger bulbs mean bigger first-year blooms. A bulb should feel firm and heavy for
its size; soft spots, mold, or a papery, dried-out feel are signs it won't perform well.
Buy from a seller with recent, positive reviews mentioning bulb quality on arrival. That's
the biggest variable in how your spring display turns out.

### Planting depth and timing

The standard rule is to plant a bulb at a depth three times its width. Most bulbs go 4-8
inches deep depending on size. In most of the US, late September through October is the
sweet spot: enough weeks of cool soil for roots to establish before the ground freezes, but
not so early that the bulb sprouts top growth before winter. Gardeners in the warmest
zones (roughly zone 8 and up) need to pre-chill tulip and hyacinth bulbs in the refrigerator
for 8-10 weeks before planting, since they need that cold period to bloom properly.

### Deer, squirrel, and vole resistance

If wildlife pressure is a real issue in your yard, lean toward daffodils and alliums. Deer
and rodents avoid both. Tulips are the most vulnerable bulb to digging
and eating; a layer of chicken wire buried a few inches above the bulbs blocks most digging
without blocking the stems from growing through.

<CalloutBox variant="tip" title="Don't cut the foliage early">
  Once bulbs finish blooming, let the leaves die back on their own instead of cutting them or
  braiding them down. The plant uses that foliage to recharge the bulb for next year's
  bloom. Cut it too soon and you'll get fewer or weaker flowers next spring.
</CalloutBox>
