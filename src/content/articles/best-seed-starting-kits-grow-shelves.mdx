Seeds need three things indoors: warmth to sprout, light to stay compact, and a tray that keeps water where it belongs. You can buy those in one box or build them from parts. This guide covers both routes and names the product we would pick for each job.

If you only want the light, our [grow lights buying guide](/best-grow-lights-buying-guide) goes deeper. For herbs on a windowsill, see the [indoor herb garden kits](/best-indoor-herb-garden-kits).

## What to Look for in a Seed Starting Setup

### Light: how long, and how close

Seedlings that sit on a windowsill in winter get too little light and stretch. Several extension services give the same rule of thumb. UConn Extension says to hold lights 2 to 3 inches above the foliage and run them 12 to 16 hours a day. Utah State Extension says 12 to 14 or more hours, and recommends a simple wall timer so you do not have to flip a switch.

You do not need a lab-grade fixture. Utah State says expensive lights marketed as "grow lights" are not necessary, and UNH Extension says LED shop lamps work fine. UNH adds that failure under lamps is more often about how much light plants get, and for how long, than about the spectrum. Pick a light you can hang low, and raise it as the plants grow.

### Heat: bottom warmth until sprouting

Most vegetable seeds sprout faster in warm soil. UConn suggests a warm spot of 70 to 75°F or a heat mat. University of Maine Extension says inexpensive mats warm the growing medium to about 70°F, and that you only need the mat until germination begins, which frees it up for the next tray. Utah State Extension adds that you should unplug a mat if the soil gets too warm.

A mat dries the soil faster, so check the moisture every day. UConn also says not to plug a heat mat into the same timer as your lights, because the mat should not be on a lighting schedule. A mat with a thermostat gives you control. A plain mat gives you no control.

### Tray design and cell count

Pick a tray size that matches what you plan to grow. A 36-cell tray suits a dozen tomatoes and a few peppers. A 60-cell tray suits a big flower or herb run. Look for drainage and a bottom tray that catches water, so you can water from below. Pop-out cells lift seedlings out with their roots intact.

### Humidity dome

A clear dome holds in moisture while seeds sprout. Check that it has vents and enough height for the seedlings you want to keep under it. Utah State Extension says that if you did not use lights during germination, you should add them once seeds sprout, so a kit that includes both a dome and a light saves a step.

### Space and footprint

A single tray fits on a counter. Three or four trays need a shelf. Measure the spot, note where the outlet is, and decide whether you want a kit that sits on a table or a rack you can park in a spare room or basement.

## Kit, Tray, or Shelf: Which Route Fits You?

**An all-in-one kit** is the fastest start. You get a tray, dome, light, and often a heat mat in one box. The trade-off is that the light is small and only covers that one tray.

**Parts** cost more but scale. A wire shelf, strip lights, and a thermostat mat let you grow several trays at once and replace any piece that breaks.

**A tray alone** is the cheapest way to start if you already have a sunny window or a light.
