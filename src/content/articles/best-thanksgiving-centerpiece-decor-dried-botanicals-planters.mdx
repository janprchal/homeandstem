Live mums and ornamental kale, the plants most people picture for a Thanksgiving table, don't really exist as a shippable product. They're grown for local garden centers and wilt fast once cut, which is why searching for them online turns up nothing but plastic. What does hold up, and what this list is actually built from, is dried and natural: real wheat, real pampas grass, real dried corn, and the vases and baskets that hold them.

If the table is doing double duty as a fall display before the meal, our guide to [indoor plants for low-light apartments](/best-indoor-succulents-low-light-apartments) covers the greenery angle separately.

## How to Put These Together

### Layering height without blocking the table

A single tall vase works fine for a small table, but a long dinner table does better with a low runner of shorter pieces, think the wood planter boxes or a shallow bowl of corn and gourds, so guests can still see and talk across the table. Save the tall wheat-and-pampas vase for a sideboard or entry table instead.

### Mixing dried pieces instead of buying one big arrangement

Wheat, pampas grass, and Indian corn are sold separately on purpose. Buying two or three of them and combining your own mix, rather than one pre-made arrangement, costs less and lets you adjust the proportions to whatever vase or basket you're actually using.

### Storing it for next year

Everything on this list is dried, not fresh, which is the whole point: pack it loosely in a box with tissue paper rather than a sealed bag that traps moisture, and it holds its shape and color for years of reuse instead of being a one-Thanksgiving purchase.

### Real produce versus decor-only pieces

The cornucopia basket is sturdy enough to hold real fruit and small pumpkins, which looks better and smells better than empty woven wicker. Buy the produce separately and swap it out through November rather than leaving the same gourds in place for weeks.
