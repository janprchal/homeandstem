Most true succulents want a sunny windowsill and sulk anywhere short of it, which makes them a bad match for a north-facing apartment or a desk in the middle of a room. The six plants below are the succulent-family exceptions: snake plant and ZZ plant actually thrive in low light, and a couple of the others tolerate it well enough to work in a dimmer corner. Two of these six are true succulents that still want more light than the rest, so we've flagged that where it matters instead of burying it in the fine print.

If you're setting these up near a desk or a reading chair, a [sturdy plant stand](/best-indoor-plant-stands-living-rooms) gets a low-light plant closer to whatever light a room does have.

## What to Look for in a Low-Light Houseplant

### How dim is "low light," actually

Low light usually means a room with a window the plant isn't sitting in front of, not a windowless closet. Snake plant and ZZ plant tolerate genuinely dim rooms; most other succulent-family plants, including several on this list, mean "no direct sun" rather than "no natural light at all."

### Watering less, not more

The plants that handle low light best also store water in thick leaves, stems, or rhizomes, which means they need less frequent watering than a typical houseplant, not more. Overwatering, not underwatering, is what kills most of the plants on this list.

### Pot drainage

A pot without a drainage hole turns "low maintenance" into "root rot" fast, especially with the reduced watering these plants prefer. Look for a nursery pot with drainage, or plan to repot into one, rather than watering carefully around a sealed decorative planter.

### Matching the plant to the actual spot

Walk the apartment and note where the light really falls before choosing a plant, not after. An interior corner with no window view calls for snake plant or ZZ plant; a windowsill that gets a few hours of indirect light can support the others, including the two true succulents here.
