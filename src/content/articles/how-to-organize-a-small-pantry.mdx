A small pantry fails in two ways: food disappears behind other food, and open bags and boxes sit around waiting for pests. A few containers and some vertical space fix both in one afternoon.

This routine takes about **2 hours** and costs **under $75** if you buy every item below. If you have a closet or a door nearby that could take more storage, our [closet organizers guide](/best-closet-organizers-small-spaces) and the [over-the-door vs. closet shelf comparison](/over-the-door-organizer-vs-closet-shelf-organizer) cover more options.

<StepBlock number={1} title="Empty the pantry and toss what's past it">
Take everything out and set it on the counter. Wipe the shelves. Then sort by what is still good. Throw out any can that is bulging, rusted, leaking, or deeply dented. Unopened commercially canned food has a recommended shelf life: Missouri Extension lists 12 to 18 months for high-acid foods like tomatoes and fruit, and 2 to 5 years for low-acid foods like meat and vegetables. Anything you know you will not eat can go to a food bank or a friend before it expires.

<CalloutBox variant="tip" title="Check where the pantry sits">
Shelf-stable food keeps best somewhere cool, dry, and dark. The Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics gives 50 to 70°F as the ideal range, and the USDA says to stay below 85°F.
</CalloutBox>
</StepBlock>

<StepBlock number={2} title="Group what is left by how you use it">
Sort the food into categories: baking, breakfast, pasta and grains, canned goods, snacks, and so on. Then place each group where you will use it. A Food & Nutrition magazine tip is to put cereal on a shelf near the bowls.
</StepBlock>

<StepBlock number={3} title="Move dry goods into airtight, labeled containers">
Illinois Extension says to cover opened food with a storage bag or a lidded container to keep pests out. Oregon State University Extension recommends glass, metal, or heavy plastic containers with tight lids, and warns that pantry insects can get through paper, plastic bags, and cardboard. Pour flour, sugar, rice, pasta, cereal, and snacks into a set of matching containers, and label each one. Matching containers stack, which saves room on narrow shelves.

<CalloutBox variant="tip" title="Keep the date with the food">
Missouri Extension suggests labeling food with the purchase date. When you decant, write the date on the label too, since the package date goes in the trash with the bag.
</CalloutBox>
</StepBlock>

<StepBlock number={4} title="Add vertical space: the door and a turntable">
A narrow pantry runs out of shelf space before it runs out of height. An over-the-door rack puts spices, packets, and small jars on the back of the door. Measure first: the Delamu listing says you need more than 5.2 inches of clearance behind the door and a door gap of more than 0.06 inches. A two-tier turntable measures 9.25 inches across and 7.7 inches tall, so it fits on a shelf and lets you spin oils, sauces, and spice jars within reach.
</StepBlock>

<StepBlock number={5} title="Restock with the oldest food in front">
Put new items behind the ones you already have. This is the first-in, first-out rule, and extension services from Clemson to Illinois recommend it. New Mexico State University suggests sorting through stored food once a month so the oldest dates stay in front and the newest at the back. After a grocery run, the new cans go behind the old ones, not on top.
</StepBlock>

## Tips and Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Don't store food above or beside the stove, under the sink, or in a damp garage or basement. The USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service warns against all of these.
- Keep food and cleaning supplies in separate areas, per Illinois Extension.
- Keep food off the floor and keep the shelves dry.
- Measure the pantry depth and door gap before you buy an over-the-door rack.
- Buy the container set after you clear the shelves, so you know how many tall and how many small containers you need.
