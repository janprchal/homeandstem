Most first gardens fail for a boring reason: too big, too shady, or planted too early. A small plan on paper fixes all three. You can do the planning in a winter afternoon, and January is the right time to start.

This guide takes about **3 hours** and the tools below cost **under $30**. For the bed itself, see our picks for [compact raised garden beds](/best-compact-raised-garden-beds-small-backyards), and for your scraps, [compost bins for small yards](/best-compost-bins-small-backyards-patios).

<StepBlock number={1} title="Find the sunniest spot you have">
Most vegetables need at least 6 hours of direct sun a day, according to extension services at Iowa State, Purdue, Illinois, and Texas A&M. NC State says fruiting crops such as tomatoes, squash, and cucumbers need at least 8 hours, and do better with 10. Leafy greens and root crops such as lettuce, radishes, and beets can get by on as little as 6.

Watch the spot on a sunny day and note when the shade starts. University of Wisconsin Extension suggests watching a site through the growing season, since trees and buildings shade it from late morning in some places. Choose a spot near the house and a water source. Iowa State says that makes watering and harvesting easier.

<CalloutBox variant="tip" title="Avoid walnut trees">
Purdue Extension notes that walnut trees make juglone, a substance that is toxic to some garden plants. Keep the bed away from them.
</CalloutBox>
</StepBlock>

<StepBlock number={2} title="Start with 25 to 50 square feet">
University of Maryland Extension says a good starter garden is 25 to 50 square feet, and you can expand later. UMass Extension says a 3 by 6 foot plot gives plenty of salad all season. A small bed you keep up beats a big one you abandon in July.

If you do not have a yard, you can grow in containers or a raised bed. Texas A&M says small, fast crops such as radishes, turnips, and beets give the most return in a small space, and that watermelons take too much room.
</StepBlock>

<StepBlock number={3} title="Pick crops you will eat, and use vertical space">
Write down the vegetables your household eats every week and start there. Nebraska Extension recommends compact varieties in a small garden. Vine crops such as cucumbers can climb a trellis or a fence, according to Illinois and Texas A&M Extension, which saves ground space.

Group crops by sun need. Virginia Tech lists tomatoes, summer squash, and Swiss chard among crops that want full sun, and beets, cabbage, and Brussels sprouts among those that tolerate partial shade.
</StepBlock>

<StepBlock number={4} title="Draw the bed on graph paper">
University of Wisconsin Extension suggests drawing the garden to scale on graph paper so every plant has room. Look up each crop's mature size on the seed packet, and mark where each crop goes. Put tall plants, such as staked tomatoes, on the north side so they do not shade the short ones. Leave space to reach every plant without stepping on the soil.

<CalloutBox variant="note" title="Test the soil first">
Your county Extension office can tell you how to send in a soil sample. A lab test reports what your soil has and what it needs. Adding compost improves soil structure, according to University of Wisconsin Extension.
</CalloutBox>
</StepBlock>

<StepBlock number={5} title="Time your planting by your last frost date">
Look up your average last spring frost date. University of Maryland Extension recommends Purdue's freeze date tool, and your county Extension office also has it. Frost dates are averages. Garden.org says the real last frost can come days or weeks before or after, so be ready to protect tender plants for a few weeks after the date.

Cool-season crops handle light frost. Garden.org lists broccoli, cabbage, cauliflower, kale, lettuce, and spinach, which can go in a few weeks before the last frost date. Utah State Extension says warm-season crops such as tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers, and squash go in after the frost danger has passed and the soil has warmed.

If you want a head start, you can sow seeds indoors. See our [guide to seed starting kits and grow shelves](/best-seed-starting-kits-grow-shelves).
</StepBlock>

<StepBlock number={6} title="Set a routine for water and weeding">
University of Maryland Extension says a vegetable garden needs at least a little attention every day: watering, staking, weeding, checking for pests, and harvesting. Put a watering can next to the bed so the job takes two minutes. Harvest often so plants keep producing.
</StepBlock>

## Tips and Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Do not plant warm-season crops the first warm week of spring. A late frost can kill them.
- Do not plant a crop that needs 8 hours of sun in a spot that gets 5.
- Do not build a bed on ground where an old building with lead paint stood. Virginia Tech warns that lead may be in the soil.
- Do not plant more than you can water. Start small and expand next year.
- Keep a simple garden notebook. You will use last year's plan for crop rotation.
